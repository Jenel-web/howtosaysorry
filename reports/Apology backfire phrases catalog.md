# Flagging thirty apology backfires, and the contexts that excuse them

Apologies mostly fail in five ways: they shift the problem onto the other person's reaction ("I'm sorry you feel that way", "sorry if"), cancel themselves ("sorry, but"), hide the actor ("mistakes were made"), demand something back (forgiveness, reciprocity, comfort), or stay too vague to show the speaker understands the harm. Expert support is strongest for six constructions: "sorry you feel/felt", "sorry if", "sorry, but", agentless "mistakes were made", generic-audience wording ("anyone who may have been offended"), and forgiveness demands. Much of the rest of the catalog (about a dozen categories, flagged below) is the researcher's own heuristic reasoning, not a sourced finding. The best-supported experiment found (Lewicki, Polin and Lount 2016, read only via secondary summaries) ranked acknowledging responsibility as the most effective apology component and requesting forgiveness as the least convincing. That finding backs the checker's design: flag what dodges responsibility, and treat everything else as a question. Of the 30 tabled patterns, 8 map onto the app's existing rules and 22 are new (each is marked in the tables), plus generic-audience wording as a further new item. Every flag should stay a dismissable question, because no source tested keyword rules like these, none reports false-positive rates, and several of the biggest situational risks (asked-for-space, abusive dynamics, legal exposure) cannot be detected from text at all.

**Evidence labels used throughout.** "Sourced" means a named expert or study supports the pattern, usually through a secondary summary, since the researchers read almost no primary papers. "Thin" means the researcher found no direct source naming the pattern as an apology failure and built it from general themes. "(illus.)" marks example phrases the researcher invented to illustrate a pattern rather than quoted from a source.

## The core of the problem: apologies that blame, condition or cancel themselves

The most-cited failures all do one thing: they leave the speaker's own act out of the sentence. A Wikipedia-based summary of the "non-apology apology" gives the standard list: "I'm sorry you feel that way" implies the listener is oversensitive, "I apologize if I offended anyone" is the "ifpology" (term credited to Harry Shearer), and John Kador argues any conditional modifier turns an apology into a non-apology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology)). Harriet Lerner, author of *Why Won't You Apologize?*, says "I'm sorry you feel that way", "I'm sorry if..." and "I'm sorry, but you have to understand..." "totally undo the apology itself" ([Mockingbird Q&A](https://mbird.com/everyday/the-art-of-a-good-apology-our-qa-with-harriet-lerner/)). She adds that "but" almost always signals a rationalization, criticism or excuse ([Mbird summary](https://mbird.com/grace-in-practice/why-wont-you-apologize/)). A therapist-site taxonomy sorts failing apologies into Deflector ("sorry you feel that way"), Minimizer ("sorry if I upset you", where "if" makes the hurt hypothetical), Conditional ("sorry, but you made me react that way"), Performer, and Transactional ("I said I'm sorry. What more do you want?") ([ReachLink](https://reachlink.com/advice/relations/why-apologies-fail/)). Wade Mullen, citing Lazare secondhand, splits pseudo-apologies into those admitting no wrongdoing ("sorry if mistakes were made") and those admitting no injury ("sorry if you were hurt") ([Substack](https://wademullen.substack.com/p/im-sorry-if)).

The empirical backbone is thinner than the expert consensus. Robbennolt's legal-settlement experiment found that the difference between a partial apology (sympathy only) and a full one is acknowledgment of fault, and that only the full version raised the odds a settlement offer would be accepted ([Michigan Law Review](https://repository.law.umich.edu/mlr/vol102/iss3/3)). No experiment on the exact strings "sorry if" or "sorry you feel" surfaced. So these rules rest on expert opinion plus the partial-apology result, which is another reason to phrase them as questions. A related, well-evidenced idea the app could use: flag a draft that expresses regret but never says what the writer did, since that is the "partial" apology Robbennolt studied (a design inference, not a tested rule).

The agentless passive needs a caution. Safire called "mistakes were made" a "passive-evasive way of acknowledging error while distancing the speaker from responsibility" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology)), but Language Log argues the popular "passive equals evasive" idea distorts the grammatical term ([Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=1539), via search summary). The rule should therefore target a short list of agentless phrases, never passive voice in general.

The researcher's own S/W suggestions are judgment calls, not findings.

Elongated or explanatory forms such as unprompted explanations belong under #13 and #17, with the draft question "Did they ask for the reason? It may land better after they've heard the apology."

## What the research does and does not support about over-apologizing and timing

The strongest instinct in the catalog, that more "sorry" is worse, is the least supported. Schumann, Ritchie and Forest (2023, hypothetical romantic scenarios, N=384 and N=300) found frequent apologizers were seen as more communal (warm, honest) and only somewhat less agentic, and only when the apologies were low quality; apology frequency did not directly change how victims reacted ([PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/are-you-a-frequent-apologizer-new-research-indicates-you-might-actually-reap-downstream-benefits)). The study tested extremes (12 versus 0 apologies), and the popular claim that over-apologizing signals incompetence has no specific citation in the notes. So a "repeated sorry" flag is defensible only as a prompt to check for substance. Tannen offers a different reading: many people's "I'm sorry" is an "automatic conversational smoother" that restores balance, and at work frequent ritual apologies can read as taking blame ([Salon](https://www.salon.com/2014/07/12/im_not_sorry_for_saying_sorry_women_should_feel_free_to_apologize_as_much_as_they_want), popular press quoting Tannen). Hence the soft wording: this is a conversational habit, not a defect.

On timing, Frantz and Bennigson (2005) found that apologies placed later in a conflict conversation worked better, mediated by the victim feeling heard; the researcher saw only abstracts and summaries ([Florida Tech summary](https://adastra.fit.edu/blog/copla/scienceminute/349-apologies-timing/)). That result concerns order within a conversation, not days or years of delay, and not sent written messages. A Melbourne study found a delayed collective apology was seen as less sincere, and an economics paper titled "Do not apologise too early" exists (abstract only), so evidence on "too soon" versus "too late" is mixed ([Melbourne](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/b9233b05-7e58-5f21-8d38-f8aa601b28d0/content); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/soceco/v90y2021ics2214804320306893.html)). This supports the app's existing pause and checklist step ("Have you listened first?") more than any text rule. No verified source supports a word-count threshold for "too long", so any length hint must be a soft question ("Is this longer than it needs to be?") and a judgment call. Finally, Fehr, Gelfand and Nag's meta-analysis of 175 studies found apology correlated about .42 with forgiveness, behind state empathy (.51) ([Ovid abstract](https://www.ovid.com/journals/plbul/pdf/10.1037/a0019993~the-road-to-forgiveness-a-meta-analytic-synthesis-of-its)). That puts apologies in the middle of forgiveness drivers, which argues against overstating what any wording tweak achieves.

## False positives: where the same words are fine

Every note in this section is the researcher's linguistic reasoning; the sources contain no false-positive study for keyword apology checkers, and none on "sorry, but I can't make it" as a polite refusal. The suppression list below should therefore be treated as a hypothesis to test against the app's own examples.

**Exempt or soften.** Sympathy phrases ("sorry for your loss", "sorry to hear that", "sorry you're going through this") are sympathy, not apology, and should not trigger the "sorry you feel" rules. This sympathy-versus-fault distinction has a real basis: most US apology statutes protect sympathy statements ([ABA TortSource 2026](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/tort_trial_insurance_practice/resources/tortsource/2026-summer/power-apology-highstakes-conflict)). By contrast, "sorry you feel that way" stays flagged because it implies the reaction is the problem ([Rice Business Wisdom](https://business.rice.edu/wisdom/features/what-makes-a-decent-apology)). Refusals ("sorry, but I can't make it", "sorry, but I have to cancel") are not apologies for harm, so the "but" flag should drop or weaken. Weaken it as well when "But" begins a new sentence after a period, or when the clause is an invitation ("I'm sorry. But I'd like to explain when you're ready"). Flag "but" most strongly when it follows an apology clause in one sentence and leads into "you..." or "I was just...". Benign conditionals ("if you're open to it", "let me know if you want space", "sorry if I'm late") should not fire; anchor "if" to sorry or apologize plus harm words. Quotation and reporting ("You said 'you always overreact', and that hurt me") contain blame words without blaming, so text inside quotation marks or after "you said" should be exempt. Negations ("I'm not saying you're overreacting") disclaim the word, though "I'm not saying X, but..." is a known pre-excuse and arguably still merits the "but" question. Self-referential absolutes ("I never meant to hurt you", "I'll always be here", "I should never have said that") should not fire the always/never rule, which belongs to absolutes aimed at the reader. "My bad" and "thanks for listening" are casual or benign and should not fire outside a serious apology context.

**Explanations.** Whether an explanation is wanted depends on whether the other person asked, which a text cannot reveal. Keep the explanation flag weak and dismissable. Likewise, after an apology has been accepted, explaining is less risky, and the tool cannot see that.

## Situational risks the text alone cannot reveal

Most of the following are plausible but unevidenced, and none should block copying. They suit the pause overlay or a general note better than a keyword rule.

**Medium and relationship.** A University of Tampa 2x2 experiment found text apologies with effective elements produced forgiveness similar to face-to-face ones, so quality mattered more than channel ([Communicating Apologies Effectively](https://utampa.dspacedirect.org/handle/20.500.11868/3402)). A secondary-source claim that couples who handle serious discussions by text are less satisfied is unverified. For the "too soon, during active conflict" worry, ask about readiness ("Has the other person had time to cool off?"), not about the medium. Workplace versus romantic versus customer-service differences (a manager may want brevity and a fix, a partner acknowledgment of feelings) are plausible but no peer-reviewed comparison was found. Per the project's out-of-scope rule on context toggles, keep one rule set and avoid universal "should" language.

**Culture and gender.** Maddux and colleagues (2011) hypothesize that in individual-agency cultures apologies assign blame, while in collective-agency cultures (Japan) they express general remorse without assigning culpability (only abstracts read) ([Harvard PON](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/international-negotiation-daily/international-negotiation-skills-before-apologizing-consider-the-culture)). Japanese "sumimasen" serves as apology, thanks and attention-getter ([CARLA bibliography](https://archive.carla.umn.edu/speechacts/japanese/apologies/apologiesbiblio.htm)). So "sorry for the trouble" and "sorry to bother you" can be ordinary politeness and should carry little or no weight, and the app should state it is tuned to English, mostly US-style norms. Schumann and Ross (2010) found women apologize more than men but apologize for the same proportion of perceived offenses; men rate offenses as less severe, so the gap tracks thresholds for what counts as an offense ([APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/when-do-you-owe-an-apology-depends-on-gender.html)). Samples were undergraduates. The implication is to use no gender-linked wording and to phrase flags as questions, since reasonable people disagree about whether an offense occurred.

**Over-apologizing in unhealthy dynamics.** Clinical commentary describes constant apologizing as a possible after-effect of trauma or a protective habit, with lower-power partners often over-apologizing and clinicians advising against simply telling survivors to stop ([DomesticShelters.org](https://www.domesticshelters.org/domestic-violence-articles-information/you-can-stop-apologizing-now)). This is observation, not controlled research, and the notes found no empirical study. A gentle prompt for text cues (three or more "sorry"s, "it's all my fault", "sorry for being too sensitive", "please don't be mad"): "Is all of this yours to own? It may be fine to apologize only for the part that was." Avoid any wording that reads as diagnosing abuse; a quiet optional resource line is safer than a flag.

**Asked for space or no contact.** Detection depends on user-typed cues ("I know you said you need space", "I know you don't want to hear from me"). The suggested question is "They asked for space. Is sending this now what they asked for?" No source addresses apology effects after a no-contact request; the rationale is respect for a stated boundary. A repair attempt that is rebuffed may escalate conflict more than none, but that claim comes from an unverified glossary entry. Offer the question in the pause overlay as well, since detection is weak.

**Public, group and legal.** For public cues ("everyone", "publicly", hashtags): "Was the harm public or private? A public apology for a private matter may expose the other person" (inference). For "we are sorry" wording: "Who is 'we'? Did everyone agree, and does the person hurt hear from someone who owns it?" (inference from corporate-apology commentary). For legal cues ("my fault", "liable", "insurance", "lawsuit"): "Could there be legal or insurance consequences? Some places treat an apology differently; a lawyer or HR can say whether this wording is okay." Roughly 36 to 39 states plus DC have apology statutes by different counts, many protecting only sympathy and mostly in medical-malpractice contexts, so the app must not give legal advice or imply apologizing is unlawful ([ABA TortSource 2026](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/tort_trial_insurance_practice/resources/tortsource/2026-summer/power-apology-highstakes-conflict); [AMA Journal of Ethics](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/im-sorry-laws-and-medical-liability/2007-04)).

**Other risks.** An apology that is prompted or coerced may be judged differently by observers than by the victim: Risen and Gilovich (2007) found observers distinguished spontaneous from coerced apologies while targets did not ([APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/jun07/insincere)). That finding concerns third-party judgment, so it adds little to the checker.

## What the app should flag, warn about, and leave alone

**New relative to the existing rules.** The new items are: took-it-that-way and other-state-as-subject variants (#3, #4, #5), relitigating (#9), "to be fair/in my defense" (#10), counter-accusations (#11), backhanded ownership (#12), agentless phrases (#15), regret-instead-of-apology (#16), intent-over-impact (#17), weasel hedges (#18), sorry-not-sorry (#19), sarcasm (#20), reciprocity demands (#22), let's-move-on (#23), self-pity (#24), apology-for-a-trade (#25), fake gratitude (#26), vague apology (#27), many sorrys (#28), no-specifics and absolute promises (#29, #30), and generic-audience wording. The repeated-sorry, vague-apology, agentless and regret items are the most sensible additions because their mechanism is clearest; weakest are #10, #12, #22, #23, #26 and #30, where the logic is the researcher's own.

**Where the notes disagree.** The two notes assign "you always/never" different strengths (weak in the catalog, strong in the context notes), and the catalog proposes "S" for several thin patterns (#10, #23, #30). A cautious setup would give strong level only to patterns with named expert backing (#1, #2, #13, #15, #21, plus the always-fine-to-flag sarcasm and sorry-not-sorry formulas, which are self-evidently non-apologies) and weak to the rest. Strong-over-weak overlap handling matters most between #1, #4, #5 and #12, and between #13, #14 and #17.

**What deserves a warning versus a flag.** Text-level flags suit the patterns above. The context risks (asked-for-space, legal, public, group, possible self-blame) are better as optional questions in the pause overlay or as a short footer, because the app cannot see who asked for what, the relationship, or the culture. The footer should state that the tool reads wording only, is tuned to English-language norms, and is not legal advice. It should make no claim that the rules are validated, since no source tests them.

## Conclusion

The research supports a narrow, well-evidenced core and a large, softer periphery. Responsibility-dodging forms (conditional, reaction-blaming, "but", agentless passive, forgiveness demands) have expert backing from Lerner, Lazare, Kador and the one experiment on component ranking, and the app already covers most of them. The new patterns the notes add (relitigating, hedges, sarcasm, self-pity, vague apology, and others) are credible but largely rest on the researcher's reasoning, so labelling them honestly as lower-confidence, weak-level questions is itself the right design.

The most surprising result is how little supports flagging over-apologizing as harmful: the one peer-reviewed study found frequent apologizers seen as warmer, which means the "many sorrys" question should ask about substance, not volume. The second is how much the real risk sits outside the text: a perfect apology sent to someone who asked for space, or one that over-owns blame in an unequal relationship, is invisible to any keyword rule. That argues for investing the remaining scope in the pause overlay's questions and honest footer rather than in more phrase rules, and for testing the suppression list (sympathy, refusals, quotes, benign "if") against the app's own examples before shipping, since no false-positive data exists.

## Evidence gaps

The researchers read almost no primary papers; most claims come from search summaries, blogs, PR-trade pieces and Wikipedia. Not opened or unavailable: Lewicki et al., Lazare's book, Lerner's book, Psychology Today (Winch), the Gottman Institute articles, NPR 2017 and 2021, and Kirchhoff et al. (2012). Not found: any source for Pamela Kaulbach, a Hargie "apology ritual", Ohbuchi's apology work, or a Brené Brown quote naming a specific backfire phrase. Kampf's 14 non-apology types were reported but not retrieved. The cited Collier page is derivative of Wikipedia, and the Chapman and Thomas "apology languages" material is popular press, not peer-reviewed. No data exist on how often each phrasing occurs in real text or on how question-form warnings affect apology quality.
