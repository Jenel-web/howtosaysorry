# Evidence and expert frameworks for apology components and backfire patterns

Verification note: all claims below come from web search snippets or fetched pages (mostly secondary: press releases, magazine/blog summaries). I read no full paper or book text. Items marked UNVERIFIED are from my background knowledge or could not be confirmed, and are not backed by a source link.

## Q1. Components of an effective apology (Lazare, Lewicki/Polin/Lount, Kirchhoff, Fehr/Gelfand/Nag, Robbennolt, Chaudhry/Loewenstein, Schumann, Ohbuchi) and which matter most

### Takeaway
Frameworks converge on a core of acknowledging responsibility, expressing regret or remorse, and offering repair. The only experimental ranking I could verify (Lewicki et al. 2016) puts acknowledgment of responsibility first and request for forgiveness last, with more components generally rated better. Partial apologies (sympathy without fault) lose their advantage over full ones in Robbennolt's legal-settlement data.

### Cited Findings
- Lazare (On Apology) describes four parts: acknowledgment (names offender and offended and fully admits the wrong), explanation (offense not deliberate or personal, unlikely to recur), remorse/shame/humility, and reparation (real or symbolic). He says "not every apology requires all four parts." — [Greater Good Science Center, "What an Apology Must Do"](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/what_an_apology_must_do); [Greater Good, "Making Peace Through Apology"](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/making_peace_through_apology)
- Lazare distinguishes reparation from a "settlement" (redress offered without acknowledged remorse). Secondary source only. — [Mediate.com](https://mediate.com/?p=95541)
- Per a Psychology Today summary of Lazare, an apology cannot demand or be contingent on the offended person's acceptance. Secondary source only. — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/feeling-our-way/201607/4-parts-real-apology)
- Lewicki, Polin & Lount (2016), "An Exploration of the Structure of Effective Apologies," Negotiation and Conflict Management Research, found six components: expression of regret, explanation of what went wrong, acknowledgment of responsibility, declaration of repentance, offer of repair, request for forgiveness. Source ordering may not match the paper's numbering. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/6-elements-effective-apology-according-science/); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/effective-apologies-include-six-elements.html)
- Lewicki et al. design: Study 1 N=333 online adults, Study 2 N=422 students. Participants read an accounting-hire scenario where the candidate's prior tax error was either a competence mistake or an integrity violation. Apologies had 1, 3 or all 6 components. They were rated on effectiveness, credibility and adequacy. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/effective-apologies-include-six-elements.html)
- Lewicki et al. results: more components meant higher ratings. Acknowledging responsibility was "by far the most effective." Offer of repair also ranked high. Request for forgiveness was least convincing in both studies. The authors say that if only one statement is possible, acknowledgment of responsibility serves best. Written apologies only. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/effective-apologies-include-six-elements.html); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/6-elements-effective-apology-according-science/)
- Fehr, Gelfand & Nag (2010), Psychological Bulletin, meta-analysis of 175 studies (N=26,006) on correlates of interpersonal forgiveness. Apology correlated about .42 with forgiveness, behind state empathy (.51) and comparable in size to state anger (-.41) and perceived intent (-.49). The search tool noted the abstract excerpt did not state sign conventions. This meta-analysis does not test which apology components work best. — [Ovid abstract](https://www.ovid.com/journals/plbul/pdf/10.1037/a0019993~the-road-to-forgiveness-a-meta-analytic-synthesis-of-its); [full text PDF via GGSC](https://ggsc.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Fehr_et_al_2010_The_road_to_forgiveness-_A_meta-analytic_synthesis_of_its_situational_and_dispositional_correlates.pdf)
- Fehr & Gelfand (2010), OBHDP, "When apologies work": apologies work best when the component matches the victim's self-construal. Two studies supported this. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v113y2010i1p37-50.html)
- Kirchhoff, Wagner & Strack (2012), "Apologies: Words of magic? The role of verbal components, anger reduction, and offense severity," Peace and Conflict 18(2), 109-130, doi 10.1037/a0028092. It builds on a 2009 review by Kirchhoff, Strack & Jäger identifying 10 components. — [PDF copy](https://vladh.net/static/books/Psychology.%20Kirchhoff%20et%20al.%20Apologies;%20Words%20of%20Magic_%20The%20Role%20of%20Verbal%20Components,%20Anger%20Reduction,%20and%20Offence%20Severity.pdf); [Psychology Today citing it](https://psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/emotional-nourishment/202204/how-and-when-should-you-say-you-are-sorry)
- Robbennolt (2003), "Apologies and Legal Settlement: An Empirical Examination," 102 Mich. L. Rev. 460. A partial apology expresses sympathy without accepting responsibility. A full apology adds acknowledgment of fault. Full-apology offenders were seen as more regretful, moral and careful in future, and drew more sympathy and forgiveness. Per a commentator, only the full apology increased the likelihood a settlement offer would be accepted. — [Michigan Law Review](https://repository.law.umich.edu/mlr/vol102/iss3/3); [Minnesota Lawyer summary](https://minnlawyer.com/2006/02/27/counseling-the-client-about-apology/)
- Robbennolt caveat: some evidence that partial apologies could create negative perceptions where the offender's responsibility was clear or the injury severe. The source is a critique by Taft, not Robbennolt's own text. — [Taft response, CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/232705458.pdf)
- Chaudhry & Loewenstein (2019), "Thanking, apologizing, bragging, and blaming: Responsibility exchange theory and the currency of communication," Psychological Review. Thanking and apologizing signal warmth and risk looking less competent. Bragging and blaming do the reverse. Receiving thanks or an apology makes the receiver seem both competent and warm. In another paper, they found transgressors overestimate the costs and underestimate the benefits of apologizing. — [CMU news](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/news/news-stories/2019/currency-of-communication.html); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/when-saying-i-m-sorry-and-thank-you-makes-big-difference)
- The "thanks vs sorry" idea in the assignment is not a paper I could locate by that name. The nearest verified source is the Chaudhry & Loewenstein Psychological Review paper above. A search for the quoted phrase "Thanks for being so understanding" returned nothing. See Gaps.
- Schumann (2014), "An affirmed self and a better apology," J. Exp. Soc. Psych. 54, 89-96: self-affirmed transgressors gave more comprehensive and less defensive apologies. Schumann (2018) review argues apologizing can threaten the transgressor's self-image as moral. — [Pitt/APS Observer summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/why-sorry-seems-to-be-the-hardest-word.html)
- Schumann (2012), J. Social & Personal Relationships, linked relationship satisfaction, perceived apology sincerity and forgiveness. — [NYU IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2012/journal-article-abstracts/does-love-mean-never-having-to-say-youre-sorry-associations-between-relationship-satisfaction-perceived-apology-sincerity-and-forgiveness/)
- Chapman & Thomas (When Sorry Isn't Enough / The 5 Apology Languages) list five "languages": express regret, accept responsibility, make restitution, genuinely repent, request forgiveness. Per the authors, 75% of surveyed couples differed in primary apology language, and using all five is the "secret" to a complete apology. Popular-press summaries, not a peer-reviewed source. — [Quick & Dirty Tips](https://quickanddirtytips.com/?p=32568); [Focus on the Family](https://www.focusonthefamily.ca/content/5-languages-of-apology); [Maria Shriver Sunday Paper](https://www.mariashriversundaypaper.com/5-apology-languages/)

### Inferences
- A checker could treat three signals as the core of a "complete" apology: clear acknowledgment of one's own action ("I did X"), regret, and repair or change. Lewicki et al. and Robbennolt support putting responsibility first. Fehr et al.'s self-construal finding suggests no single recipe fits everyone, which supports dismissable, question-style flags.
- Lewicki et al. rated request for forgiveness lowest, and Lazare says an apology cannot be contingent on acceptance. A "please forgive me" flag is therefore reasonable but should be the softest one. Note the study measured perceived effectiveness of text, not real relationships.
- Chapman & Thomas include "request forgiveness" as a legitimate language, so the evidence on it is mixed. Phrase any flag as a question.

### Gaps
- Kirchhoff et al. (2012) specific results: the one full-text PDF I fetched came back as binary and I could not read it. I cannot say which verbal components predicted anger reduction or how offense severity moderated effects.
- Ohbuchi (1989, apology and aggression/anger reduction): no source found. UNVERIFIED. I recall (not verified) that Ohbuchi, Kameda & Agarie (1989) found apologies reduced aggression via perceived sincerity or responsibility. Do not cite without checking.
- Kirchhoff, Strack & Jäger (2009) list of 10 components: not retrieved.
- Chaudhry & Loewenstein "thanks vs sorry" as a self-focus-in-apology finding: could not verify beyond the Psychological Review paper. A textbook table that labels admission, regret and restitution as "self-focused" components was surfaced by search, but I did not verify its source.
- Lazare's own text: all views are via Greater Good and secondary summaries, with no page numbers.

## Q2. What Brené Brown, Harriet Lerner and Chapman/Thomas say about failed apologies (specific phrases)

### Takeaway
The specific phrase warnings come from Lerner, not Brown. Lerner flags "I'm sorry you feel that way," "I'm sorry if...," and "I'm sorry, but...," plus defensive explaining and listing the other person's faults. Chapman/Thomas frame failure as a mismatch of apology language, not as particular phrases.

### Cited Findings
- Lerner (Why Won't You Apologize?, 2017) says these caveats "totally undo the apology itself": "I'm sorry you feel that way," "I'm sorry if...," and "I'm sorry, but you have to understand..." Vague or obfuscating language "obscures what we are sorry for." — [Mockingbird Q&A with Lerner](https://mbird.com/everyday/the-art-of-a-good-apology-our-qa-with-harriet-lerner/)
- A good apology is free of "a hint of evasion, blaming, obfuscation, or excuse-making." Lerner warns against going into debate mode to correct the facts, and against bringing up the other person's "crime sheet"; save your side for a second conversation. — [Mockingbird Q&A](https://mbird.com/everyday/the-art-of-a-good-apology-our-qa-with-harriet-lerner/)
- In an NPR interview, Lerner says the word "but" almost always signals a rationalization, criticism or excuse, and calls "I'm sorry if you feel that way" very misguided. — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2017/11/22/565913664/how-to-apologize-for-sexual-harassment-hint-it-takes-more-than-sorry)
- Lerner attributes difficulty apologizing largely to defensiveness ("Humans are wired for defensiveness"). Per a podcast summary, people who equate "I did something wrong" with "I'm a bad person" fall into shame too fast to apologize. — [Brené Brown, Unlocking Us, Lerner part 2](https://brenebrown.com/podcast/im-sorry-how-to-apologize-why-it-matters-part-2-of-2/)
- Lerner's point that a heartfelt apology can still fall flat if the receiver does not feel heard comes from a search summary of the Brown/Lerner podcast. — [Unlocking Us summary](https://metacast.app/podcast/unlocking-us-with-brene-brown/mjN7WwZY/harriet-lerner-and-brene---i-m-sorry-how-to-apologize-and-why-it-matters-part-2-of-2/jmsuWvaG)
- Brené Brown: I found no primary-source quote where she names a specific backfire phrase. Her 2020 Unlocking Us two-parter is built on Lerner's book. — [Unlocking Us part 2](https://brenebrown.com/podcast/im-sorry-how-to-apologize-why-it-matters-part-2-of-2/)
- Chapman & Thomas: without restitution, the receiver will question the sincerity of the apology. Their "failure" mechanism is language mismatch (e.g., offering regret to someone who needs restitution). — [Focus on the Family](https://www.focusonthefamily.ca/content/5-languages-of-apology)
- Secondary sources attribute to John Kador (Effective Apology) the view that adding "if" or any conditional modifier turns an apology into a non-apology. Not checked against the book. — [Wikipedia-derived page on non-apology apology](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauxpology)

### Inferences
- Rules for "sorry you feel/felt," "sorry if," "sorry but/however," and "but you" can cite Lerner. They should not be attributed to Brown.
- Lerner's warning about "crime sheet" and defensive correction suggests flagging blame-shifting phrases ("you made me," "if you hadn't"). The source describes the behavior, not exact strings; the strings are my inference.

### Gaps
- Lerner's book text: not read; quotes are from interviews and Q&A.
- Chapman/Thomas specific "phrases to avoid": not found. Their "I'm sorry" / "I was wrong" examples came from one interview summary.
- Whether Brown has a signature warning (e.g., "I'm sorry you're upset"): not verified.

## Q3. Over-apologizing, length, timing, and requesting forgiveness

### Takeaway
Evidence that over-apologizing hurts the apologizer is thin and mixed. The one peer-reviewed study I found (Schumann et al. 2023) found frequent apologizers seen as warmer, only somewhat less assertive, and only when apologies were low quality. Timing evidence (Frantz & Bennigson 2005) favors apologies placed later in a conversation, after the victim has been heard. Request for forgiveness rated least convincing (Lewicki et al.). I found no verified study on apology length.

### Cited Findings
- Schumann, Ritchie & Forest (2023), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin. Study 1 N=384, Study 2 N=300 in romantic relationships; hypothetical scenarios. Frequent apologizers were seen as more communal (warm, honest) and somewhat less agentic (assertive, confident). Frequency did not directly change how victims reacted to an apology. In Study 2, only low-quality frequent apologies reduced perceived assertiveness. Caveat: tested extremes (12 vs 0 apologies), not real apologies. — [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/are-you-a-frequent-apologizer-new-research-indicates-you-might-actually-reap-downstream-benefits)
- Schumann & Ross: women apologized more than men in daily life, but women also reported more offenses, which fully accounted for the difference. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/are-you-a-frequent-apologizer-new-research-indicates-you-might-actually-reap-downstream-benefits)
- Chaudhry & Loewenstein: apologizing and thanking carry a warmth/competence trade-off, so people are more reluctant to apologize where appearing competent matters. — [CMU news](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/news/news-stories/2019/currency-of-communication.html)
- Popular claims that over-apologizing signals incompetence (attributed to Cuddy, Tannen) lack specific citations in the sources I found. Treat as unverified. — [Substack piece](https://laylool.substack.com/p/your-apologies-are-costing-you-credibility)
- Frantz & Bennigson (2005), "Better late than early," J. Exp. Soc. Psych. 41(2), 201-207. Study 1 used real conflicts, Study 2 had 83 participants reading hypothetical conflicts with the apology at beginning, end or absent. Later apologies were more effective, mediated by the victim feeling heard and understood. Full text not accessed. — [Abstract record](https://opact.sdu.edu.tr/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c454t-8883848902e9b013edc4d8b41ab72d513bdb036d1b4781f4ab51eb6ae6c80a703/Description); [Florida Tech summary](https://adastra.fit.edu/blog/copla/scienceminute/349-apologies-timing/)
- Counterview: a Wikipedia article says attribution theory predicts earlier apologies lead to less conflict, and that apologies "too little, too late" can backfire. Weak source. — [Wikipedia, Apology (act)](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apologise)
- Request for forgiveness: lowest-rated of six components in both Lewicki et al. studies (see Q1). Lazare: apology cannot be contingent on acceptance. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/effective-apologies-include-six-elements.html)
- Risen & Gilovich (2007), JPSP 92, 418-433: in staged-offense studies, observers distinguished spontaneous from coerced apologies but targets (the victims) did not. Relevance to this app: indicates apologies prompted by others may be judged differently by third parties, not that coerced apologies fail with victims. — [PsyArxiv-style abstract via SAGE/Primo](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_70263691); [APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/jun07/insincere)

### Inferences
- No evidence-based word-count threshold exists in what I found. A length hint should be framed softly ("Is this longer than it needs to be?") and not as a verdict.
- A "repeated sorry" flag is defensible only as a prompt to check whether the apology has substance (Schumann 2023: low-quality frequent apologies were the ones that cost perceived assertiveness). It should not claim over-apologizing erodes forgiveness.
- Because later apologies worked better when the victim had been heard, a pause/checklist step ("Have you listened first?") fits the evidence. The finding is from conflict discussions, not from sent written messages.

### Gaps
- Over-apologizing: no study found showing over-apologizing reduces apology acceptance.
- Length/verbosity: no verified source. Including any "too long" rule is a judgment call.
- Timing "too late": Frantz & Bennigson address early vs late within a conversation, not days or years of delay.
- Frantz & Bennigson effect sizes: not retrieved.

## Q4. Conditional apologies, "sorry you feel," "sorry but," and non-apologies (Kaulbach, Kampf, Risen & Gilovich, Hargie, Wohl/Hornsey)

### Takeaway
Experts (Lazare, Lerner, Kador) agree conditional and "sorry you feel" forms read as non-apologies. Empirical backing is stronger for the broader principle that full, responsibility-accepting apologies outperform partial ones (Robbennolt 2003). I could not verify Kaulbach, Hargie, or a Kampf finding on "if" or "but," and no experiment on "sorry you feel" surfaced.

### Cited Findings
- Lazare's critique of "I apologize if I offended anyone," as summarized on a blog about the book: the word "if" implies perhaps no one was really offended, and implies the offense is the sensitive person's problem. Secondary source. — [Experimental Theology blog](https://experimentaltheology.blogspot.com/2006/06/theology-of-everyday-life-on-apology.html)
- Wade Mullen (citing Lazare, secondary) separates two pseudo-apologies: ones that admit no wrongdoing ("I'm sorry if mistakes were made") and ones that admit no injury ("I'm sorry if you were hurt"). — [Substack](https://wademullen.substack.com/p/im-sorry-if)
- Robbennolt (2003): the key difference between full and partial apology is admission of responsibility. Partial apologies did not raise settlement acceptance. — [Michigan Law Review](https://repository.law.umich.edu/mlr/vol102/iss3/3)
- Kampf: a Miller-McCune piece (reposted by APS) reports Zohar Kampf identified 14 ways to offer a non-apology apology, from studies between 1997 and 2004. I could not verify the typology itself. — [APS repost](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=62349)
- Non-apology apology: a statement in apology form that expresses no remorse or blames the recipient. Encyclopedic, common in politics and PR. — [Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauxpology)
- Stein & Barton (2018, per a Southern Utah University news item): public figures are often credited with an apology even when their language suggests lack of remorse. — [SUU news](https://suu.edu/news/2018/10/communication-professors-publish-research.html)
- Wohl, Hornsey & Philpot (2011), Social Issues and Policy Review 5, 70-100: critical review of official public apologies with a "staircase model." Later work cited in search results says apologies are more effective at achieving satisfaction than forgiveness (citing Hornsey & Wohl 2013), and that a 2012 study (Wohl, Hornsey & Bennett) found an apology expressing secondary emotions aroused no more forgiveness than no apology. These concern group apologies, not individual written ones. — [Cardiff repository copy](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/138548/2/ejsp.2758.pdf); [SAMA record](https://samafind.sama.gov.sa/index.php/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........419bef3aabe78cb5ddd3f43945d6d180/Preview)
- Risen & Gilovich (2007): see Q3. It concerns questionable (coerced) apologies, not conditional wording.

### Inferences
- Rules for "if," "sorry you feel," "sorry but" rest on expert opinion (Lazare, Lerner) plus the Robbennolt partial-apology result, not on direct experiments of those exact strings. Question-style flags are appropriate given that evidential level.
- "Partial" status (sympathy only, no "I did") is the best-evidenced thing to detect: flag apologies with regret but no first-person ownership of the act.

### Gaps
- Pamela Kaulbach: nothing found. UNVERIFIED; I cannot say what she wrote or confirm the name.
- Hargie "apology ritual": nothing found.
- Kampf's 14-category list and any finding on conditional "if": not retrieved.
- Wohl/Hornsey on partial or conditional apologies specifically: not found; the 2011 review's treatment of "if" is unconfirmed.
- Any experiment on "sorry you feel/felt this way": not found.
- Ohbuchi, Kader: not found (name "Kader" may be a mis-spelling; I could not identify a non-apology study by that name).
