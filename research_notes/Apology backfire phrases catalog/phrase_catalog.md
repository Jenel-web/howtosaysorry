# Apology backfire phrases catalog (English)

Conventions: "Cited Findings" carry sources. Example phrases marked (illus.) are my own realistic illustrations of a pattern, not quotes from a source. Detection notes are in plain words, under Inferences, because they are my design suggestions rather than sourced facts. Most source support is secondary (blogs, therapist sites, PR trade pieces, Wikipedia); few primary studies were reachable.

## Q1. Which phrases do experts and journalists most often cite as non-apologies or apology failures?

### Takeaway
Four constructions recur across sources: "I'm sorry you feel that way" (blaming the recipient), "I'm sorry if..." (conditional "ifpology"), "I'm sorry, but..." (excuse or blame), and "mistakes were made" (agentless passive). Corporate/generic-audience forms ("anyone who may have been offended", "any inconvenience") and demands for forgiveness ("I said I'm sorry, what more do you want?") are the next most-cited.

### Cited Findings
- "I'm sorry you feel that way" implies the listener is overly sensitive; "I apologize if I offended anyone" is the "ifpology" (term credited to Harry Shearer); John Kador argues any conditional modifier makes an apology a non-apology — [Wikipedia: Non-apology apology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology)
- Generic-audience form: "to anyone who might have been offended" does not address the injured party; "I regret that people were offended" regrets the outcome, not the behavior; "Nobody is sorrier than me that..." sounds regretful without accepting blame — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology); [Collier summary page](https://jamescollier7.github.io/non-apology/) (that page cites no sources of its own and says it is based on Wikipedia and unnamed studies, so treat it as derivative)
- "Mistakes were made": Safire called it a "passive-evasive way of acknowledging error while distancing the speaker from responsibility for it"; William Schneider proposed the label "past exonerative" tense; NYT called it a "classic Washington linguistic construct" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology). NPR notes its use across the Nixon, Reagan, Clinton and Bush eras — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/blogs/thetwo-way/2013/05/14/183924858/its-true-mistakes-were-made-is-the-king-of-non-apologies) (as summarized by search result; page not opened)
- Harriet Lerner (author of *Why Won't You Apologize?*): "I'm sorry if..." and "I'm sorry, but..." are non-apologies; "'But' almost always signifies a rationalization, a criticism, or an excuse"; NPR reports she called John Lasseter's "I deeply apologize if I have left you down" an "obfuscating, gaslighting" model (search-snippet summary) — [Mbird summary of Lerner](https://mbird.com/grace-in-practice/why-wont-you-apologize/); [NPR 2017](https://www.npr.org/2017/11/22/565913664/how-to-apologize-for-sexual-harassment-hint-it-takes-more-than-sorry) (NPR page timed out on fetch; claim from search summary only)
- A therapist-site taxonomy of failing apologies: Deflector "I'm sorry you feel that way"; Minimizer "I'm sorry if I upset you" (the "if" makes hurt seem hypothetical); Conditional "I'm sorry, but you made me react that way" (everything after "but" cancels the apology and shifts blame); Performer (emotional display centering the apologizer's distress, pressuring the hurt person to reassure); Transactional "I said I'm sorry. What more do you want?" (expects immediate forgiveness) — [ReachLink](https://reachlink.com/advice/relations/why-apologies-fail/)
- "I'm sorry I hurt you" alone is criticized as non-specific; the injured person needs to see you understand the offense — [GoodTherapy](https://www.goodtherapy.org/blog/?p=23759) (search-summary only)
- Justifying behavior or blaming the partner inside an apology is a commonly flagged failure — [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/emotional-nourishment/202204/how-and-when-should-you-say-you-are-sorry) (search-summary only)
- PR sources: conditional corporate wording ("sorry if anyone was offended", "We apologise to anyone this may have offended" attributed to H&M, "for any inconvenience this situation may have caused" attributed to an Apple email) apologizes for the audience's reaction, not the action; "we regret" is about the speaker's feeling, not the other's — [5WPR glossary](https://www.5wpr.com/glossary/non-apology/), [Everything-PR](https://everything-pr.com/the-corporate-apology-is-dying-good), [InformationWeek](https://www.informationweek.com/it-infrastructure/apple-s-top-20-public-apologies) (all via search summaries; trade/PR-firm opinion)
- Request for forgiveness was the lowest-rated of six apology components; acknowledging responsibility rated highest, offer of repair second — Lewicki, Polin & Lount 2016, via [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/04/160412091111.htm) and [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/minds-business/effective-apologies-include-six-elements.html) (secondary summaries; paper not opened). This supports flagging forgiveness-pressure.

### Inferences
- The strongest, best-supported flag set for a v1 checker is: "sorry you feel/felt", "sorry if", "sorry, but", agentless "mistakes were made", "anyone who may have been offended", and forgiveness demands.
- Everything beyond that list (reciprocity demands, fake gratitude, sorry-not-sorry) is real in expert advice or common usage but has thinner citation in what I could reach.

### Gaps
- I could not open Psychology Today (Winch) or the Gottman Institute article (Anubis block / no body text); no primary Gottman source found on "if I hurt you". One secondary blog attributes a Gottman claim about conditional apologies but I could not verify it, so I omit it.
- NPR 2017 and NPR 2021 fetches timed out; only search-snippet summaries available.

## Q2. What do linguists and researchers say about these constructions?

### Takeaway
Scholarship supports typologies (tactical, explanation, formalistic, "happy ending") and a clear link between non-apologies and missing acknowledgement of responsibility; Tannen's work reframes frequent "I'm sorry" as a conversational ritual (relevant to over-apologizing), and Language Log cautions against equating grammatical passive with evasion. I found no "Edwards" non-apology study or a Jennifer Thomas source.

### Cited Findings
- Deborah Levi's typology (via Grigsby 2007): tactical, explanation, formalistic, and "happy ending" (full responsibility) apologies — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology)
- Aaron Lazare (*On Apology*, 2004): pseudo-apologies can express real regret but can also elicit forgiveness without acknowledging fault; Guy Winch: tactical apologies validate feelings while protecting the apologizer from remorse; Fisher and Ury: an apology can defuse emotions even without admitting responsibility — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology)
- Lazare's effective-apology frame is commonly summarized as recognition, responsibility, remorse, reparation (secondary calendar-site summary; other authors give five-part variants: Kador: recognition, responsibility, remorse, restitution, repetition) — [summary](https://www.calendar-uk.co.uk/frequently-asked-questions/what-are-the-4-rs-of-the-ideal-apology), search-result summary only
- Deborah Tannen: many women's (and some men's) "I'm sorry" works as an "automatic conversational smoother" and ritual to restore balance rather than a literal admission of fault; ritual apologizers expect one in return (apology-for-apology exchange); at work frequent ritual apologies can read as taking blame — [Salon](https://www.salon.com/2014/07/12/im_not_sorry_for_saying_sorry_women_should_feel_free_to_apologize_as_much_as_they_want), [Spokesman-Review 1997](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/1997/mar/16/im-sorry-experts-says-men-women-tend-to-approach) (popular press quoting Tannen; check *Talking from 9 to 5* for formal citation)
- Language Log: "mistakes were made" is passive in both traditional and popular senses and is evasive enough to anger people wanting contrition; an agentless passive appeared in PwC's Oscars statement; but Language Log posts also argue the popular "passive = evasive" notion distorts the grammatical term — [Language Log: Mistakes were made](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=1246), [PwC passives](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=31303), ["Passive construction" means... nothing at all?](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=1539) (search-summary only)
- Academic pragmatics: a UWE study (N=78 British English speakers) on responses to "I'm sorry" treats non-apologies as a mismatch between formula and apology; a McMaster MSc thesis (Beaudin 2018) finds many public apologies felicitous despite using non-apology strategies — [UWE repository](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/849850), [McMaster thesis](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/bitstream/11375/24278/2/Beaudin_Laura_M_2018Sept_MSc.pdf) (search summaries; not opened)

### Inferences
- Because passive voice is not inherently evasive (Language Log), a checker should flag only agentless-error phrases ("mistakes were made", "errors occurred") and word the flag as a question, not flag all passives.
- Tannen's ritual reading means over-apologizing flags should be framed softly ("Does repeating sorry help here?"), not as a defect.

### Gaps
- No source found for a linguist named Edwards on non-apologies, nor for Jennifer Thomas on apology (Jenny Thomas wrote on pragmatic failure, not verified here). Marked unfound, not fabricated.
- Primary papers (Lewicki et al., Levi, Lazare) were not read directly.

## Q3. Categorized catalog with examples, why it backfires, and detection wording

### Takeaway
30 patterns are listed. Categories 1-7, 9-10, 12-13, 20, 25-26 and 30 have expert or reference backing (see citations); several others are thin. Others are drawn from standard apology-advice themes and my own illustrations; those are labelled "support: thin" so the writer does not overstate them.

### Cited Findings
Citation per category (the example phrases are in Inferences). Only categories with sources I actually saw are listed here.
1. Sorry-but excuses: "But" signals rationalization or excuse (Lerner) — [Mbird](https://mbird.com/grace-in-practice/why-wont-you-apologize/); everything after "but" cancels the apology and shifts blame — [ReachLink](https://reachlink.com/advice/relations/why-apologies-fail/)
2. "Sorry you feel/felt": blames the recipient's sensitivity — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology), [ReachLink](https://reachlink.com/advice/relations/why-apologies-fail/)
3. Conditional "if": "if" casts doubt on whether hurt occurred — [ReachLink](https://reachlink.com/advice/relations/why-apologies-fail/); Kador via [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology)
4. Regretting the outcome / reaction ("sorry you took it that way", "regret people were offended") — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology), [Collier](https://jamescollier7.github.io/non-apology/)
5. Passive / no agent ("mistakes were made") — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology), [Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=1246), [NPR](https://www.npr.org/blogs/thetwo-way/2013/05/14/183924858/its-true-mistakes-were-made-is-the-king-of-non-apologies)
6. Blame shifting ("you made me"): [ReachLink](https://reachlink.com/advice/relations/why-apologies-fail/); blaming partner within apology — [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/emotional-nourishment/202204/how-and-when-should-you-say-you-are-sorry)
7. Pressure for forgiveness / "I said sorry": [ReachLink](https://reachlink.com/advice/relations/why-apologies-fail/); request for forgiveness is the lowest-rated component — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/04/160412091111.htm)
8. Self-focused performance ("I feel terrible"): "Performer" pattern — [ReachLink](https://reachlink.com/advice/relations/why-apologies-fail/); apologies should focus on the other's needs, not yours (Winch, via search summary of [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/ie/blog/the-squeaky-wheel/201311/the-five-ingredients-effective-apology))
9. Vague apology ("sorry for everything"): non-specific apology weakens it — [GoodTherapy](https://www.goodtherapy.org/blog/?p=23759)
10. Over-apologizing: Tannen's ritual-apology analysis — [Salon](https://www.salon.com/2014/07/12/im_not_sorry_for_saying_sorry_women_should_feel_free_to_apologize_as_much_as_they_want)
11. Corporate / generic audience: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology), [5WPR](https://www.5wpr.com/glossary/non-apology/), [Everything-PR](https://everything-pr.com/the-corporate-apology-is-dying-good)
12. Sarcastic apology: Bruce McCall parody type — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-apology_apology)
Not found with a direct source: minimizing ("overreacting", "too sensitive", "just a joke", "calm down" as a named apology failure), "you always/never", reciprocity, relitigating, "let's move on", vague/over-promising, fake gratitude, "to be fair/in my defense", weasel hedges, "sorry not sorry". Their backfire logic is covered in Inferences and is flagged "support: thin".

### Inferences
Each entry: examples (illus. unless a source quote), why it backfires, and how a checker could detect it in words. Strength suggestion is S (strong flag) or W (weak flag) per the project's rule that strong beats weak on overlap.

**1. Sorry-but excuses (S).** Examples: "I'm sorry, but I was stressed." / "Sorry, however you also..." / "I apologize, but you have to understand..." / "I'm sorry but that's just how I am." Backfires: the clause after "but" negates the apology and reads as excuse or criticism. Detect: a sorry/apologize/apologies word followed within a few words (a comma optional) by "but", "however", "although", "except", "though", "yet"; also "..., but" later in the same sentence when the sentence begins with an apology. Also catch "sorry, it's just that" and "I'm sorry. But" across a sentence break.

**2. Sorry you feel/felt/were upset (S).** Examples: "I'm sorry you feel that way." / "Sorry you felt hurt." / "I'm sorry you were upset." / "I'm sorry that you're offended." / "I regret that you're upset." Backfires: apologizes for the other's emotion, not my action; implies oversensitivity. Detect: sorry/apologize/regret + (that) + "you" + feel/felt/feeling/are/were/got/become + an emotion word (upset, hurt, offended, angry, mad, bothered, uncomfortable), or "you feel that way". Variants include "I'm sorry for how you feel" and "sorry that it made you feel".

**3. Conditional "if" (S).** Examples: "I'm sorry if I hurt you." / "I apologize if anything I said offended anyone." / "Sorry if that came across wrong." / "If I did anything wrong, I'm sorry." / "I'm sorry in case it upset you." Backfires: questions whether harm happened; shifts to the other's reaction. Detect: sorry/apologize/regret followed by "if", "in case", "should", "to the extent that", "whether"; also an opening "if I ... , I'm sorry/I apologize". Add "if it matters" / "if that helps". Special care: "sorry if" can be a benign real phrase ("sorry if I'm late"), so flag the harm-related ones (hurt, offend, upset, wrong) as S and others as W.

**4. Sorry you took it that way / reaction regret (S).** Examples: "I'm sorry you took it that way." / "Sorry you interpreted it like that." / "I'm sorry it came across that way." / "I regret that my words were misunderstood." / "Sorry you misunderstood." Backfires: the problem becomes the listener's interpretation. Detect: sorry/regret + "you" + took/took it/interpreted/read/misunderstood/misread/heard it; or "it came across", "was taken", "was misunderstood", "was perceived".

**5. Passive / no agent (S for named phrases, W otherwise).** Examples: "Mistakes were made." / "Errors were committed." / "Things were said." / "Feelings got hurt." / "It was unfortunate that this happened." / "There was a misunderstanding." Backfires: admits error but names no actor (Safire's "passive-evasive"). Detect: a short list of agentless phrases: "mistakes were made", "mistakes have been made", "errors were made", "things were said", "words were said", "feelings were hurt", "there was a misunderstanding", "it happened", "this occurred", "it was unfortunate that". Do not flag all passives (Language Log caution).

**6. Blame shifting (S).** Examples: "You made me do it." / "I only yelled because you..." / "If you hadn't ignored me, I wouldn't have..." / "It's your fault too." / "You pushed my buttons." / "You drove me to it." Backfires: casts the recipient as cause (ReachLink: the "Conditional" type). Detect: "you made me", "you forced me", "you drove me to", "you pushed my buttons", "you caused", "because you" (W alone, S when the clause follows an apology), "if you hadn't", "your fault", "you started it", "you're the one who", "look what you made me".

**7. Minimizing (S for dismissive words, W for "just").** Examples: "You're overreacting." / "You're too sensitive." / "It was just a joke." / "It's not that big a deal." / "Calm down." / "Don't take it so seriously." / "Get over it." / "I was only kidding." Backfires: tells the person their hurt is invalid (support: thin as a labelled non-apology category, but widely cited in conflict advice; the Wikipedia "sensitive recipient" framing supports the core idea). Detect: overreact(ing/ed), "too sensitive", "so sensitive", "being sensitive", "just a joke", "just joking", "only a joke", "only kidding", "not a big deal", "no big deal", "not that bad", "calm down", "get over it", "blow(n) out of proportion", "make a big deal", "drama", "dramatic", "it's not like", "relax".

**8. You always / you never (W).** Examples: "You always twist my words." / "You never listen, that's why I snapped." / "Everyone knows you take things the wrong way." / "You're always like this." Backfires: generalization turns the apology into an accusation (support: thin; standard in Gottman-style "criticism" advice, not verified here). Detect: "you always", "you never", "you constantly", "you're always", "you're never", "every time you", especially in the same message as an apology.

**9. Vague apologies (W).** Examples: "Sorry for everything." / "I'm sorry for whatever I did." / "Sorry for any trouble." / "I'm sorry about what happened." / "Sorry for all of it." Backfires: shows no understanding of the specific harm (GoodTherapy). Detect: sorry/apologize + "for everything", "for whatever", "for anything", "for all", "for what happened", "about what happened", "about all that", "for that thing", "for being me". Also flag the whole text if the only sorry is followed by no concrete noun/verb (hard to detect; use the length hint).

**10. Pressure for forgiveness (S).** Examples: "Forgive me already." / "I said I'm sorry." / "What more do you want?" / "So are we good now?" / "Can we just be done with this?" / "How many times do I have to say sorry?" / "You have to forgive me." Backfires: turns the apology into a transaction; forgiveness is the recipient's to give (ReachLink "Transactional"; Lewicki et al. rate request for forgiveness lowest). Detect: "I said (I'm) sorry", "I already apologized", "what more do you want", "what else do you want", "are we good", "we're good", "we good", "forgive me" with "already/now/just", "you have to forgive", "you should forgive", "how many times", "can we move past", "stop being mad", "get over it". Note: a soft single "I hope you can forgive me someday" is mild; flag only W.

**11. Demanding reciprocity (S).** Examples: "I'll apologize if you do." / "I'll say sorry when you say sorry." / "I'm sorry, now you say it." / "We were both wrong, so..." / "You owe me an apology too." / "Fair is fair, you apologize first." Backfires: conditions remorse on the other (support: thin as a cited category; Tannen notes ritual reciprocity exchange expectation, which is a related but different pattern). Detect: "if you apologize", "when you apologize", "if you say sorry", "you owe me", "you should apologize too", "you also need to apologize", "we both", "your turn", "now you".

**12. Self-pity / self-focus (W).** Examples: "I feel so terrible, I'm such a bad person." / "I'm the worst." / "I can't sleep, I'm so ashamed." / "I hate myself for this." / "You know how hard this is for me." Backfires: recipient must comfort the offender (ReachLink "Performer"; Winch: focus on the other's feelings). Detect: first-person emotion/identity self-labels: "I feel (so) terrible/awful/guilty/horrible/bad/ashamed", "I'm (such) a (terrible|bad|horrible) person", "I'm the worst", "I hate myself", "I'm an idiot", "poor me", "this is hard for me", "I can't live with myself"; high counts of "I/me/my" relative to "you/your" can be a secondary signal (a count is easy; keep as W).

**13. Over-apologizing / groveling (W).** Examples: "I'm so so sorry, I'm really, really sorry, I'm so sorry." / "I'm beyond sorry, I'll do anything." / "Please please forgive me, I beg you." / "I'm sorry sorry sorry." Backfires: the effusion shifts attention to my distress and can feel insincere or burdensome; Tannen shows "sorry" as ritual can read as taking too much blame. Detect: three or more sorry/apologize forms within ~60 words (or repeated "sorry" back-to-back); stacked intensifiers ("so so sorry", "really really"); "I beg you", "please forgive me", "I'll do anything", "I'll do whatever it takes" (the last also an overpromise, see 19).

**14. Defensiveness and justification (S for explicit intent phrases).** Examples: "I was only trying to help." / "My intention was never to hurt you." / "I didn't mean it." / "I meant well." / "That wasn't my intent." / "You know I'm not like that." Backfires: intent talk centers my self-image over impact; impact is what matters (support: thin; common in expert advice such as Lazare/Lerner themes but not directly quoted in what I read). Detect: "I was (just|only) trying to", "I only wanted to", "my intention(s) (was|were)", "my intent", "I didn't mean to" (W alone; fine when paired with owning impact), "I meant (well|no harm)", "I never meant", "I'm not (that kind of person|like that)", "you know me", "that's not who I am".

**15. Relitigating ("sorry, but that's not what happened") (S).** Examples: "I'm sorry, but that's not what happened." / "That's not how I remember it." / "Actually, what I said was..." / "You're misremembering." / "Let me explain what really happened." Backfires: re-argues the facts while claiming to apologize; invalidates the other's account. Detect: "that's not what happened", "that's not what I said", "that's not how it happened", "not how I remember", "you're (misremembering|misquoting|twisting|exaggerating)", "what really happened", "the truth is", "actually," near a sorry; "let me explain" (W).

**16. Let's move on / forget it (S).** Examples: "Let's just move on." / "Forget it." / "Let's put this behind us." / "Water under the bridge." / "Can we drop it?" / "Let's not dwell on it." / "It's in the past." Backfires: cuts off the other person's chance to respond; prioritizes my comfort. Detect: "move on", "move past", "forget it", "forget about it", "drop it", "let it go", "put this behind us", "water under the bridge", "in the past", "dwell on", "bring it up again", "never speak of".

**17. Promises with no specifics (W).** Examples: "I'll try to do better." / "It won't happen again." / "I'll change, I promise." / "I'll be better." Backfires: unverifiable and unspecific; the offered repair component rates high, but only when concrete (Lewicki et al., via secondary summary). Detect: "I'll (try to )?do better", "I'll be better", "I'll change", "I promise (to change|I'll)", "it won't happen again", "from now on" with no following concrete verb phrase; a cheap rule is to flag these when no "I will + specific verb + object" occurs.

**18. Overpromising absolutes (S).** Examples: "I'll never do it again." / "I'll never let you down again." / "I will always be there." / "I promise I'll never get angry." / "I swear it will never happen again." Backfires: absolute promises are quickly broken and sound like pressure for trust. Detect: "never (ever )?(do|say|let|make|hurt|be|happen)", "never again", "will always", "I swear", "I guarantee", "100%", "I promise (you )?(I'll )?never", "whatever it takes", "anything you want".

**19. Fake gratitude (W).** Examples: "Thanks for putting up with me." / "Thanks for being so understanding as always." / "Thank you for your patience, I guess." / "Thanks for hearing me out, finally." / "I appreciate you not blowing up." Backfires: reads as sarcastic or a bid for praise; gratitude replaces ownership (support: thin; my observation). Detect: "thanks for putting up with", "thanks for (being|dealing|tolerating|bearing)", "thank you for putting up", "thank you for your patience" near a sorry; "thanks for nothing". Benign "thanks for listening" should be W or not flagged.

**20. Sarcasm (S for formula, W otherwise).** Examples: "Well, excuse me for having feelings." / "Sorry for breathing." / "I'm sooo sorry that I'm such a terrible person." / "Oh, I'm sorry, did I hurt your feelings?" / "Sorry I'm not perfect like you." / "My bad, your majesty." Backfires: openly mocks the apology (Bruce McCall's parody type, per Wikipedia). Detect: "sorry for breathing", "excuse me for", "sorry I'm not perfect", "sorry (that )?I('m| am) such a", "sooo+ sorry", "sorry (your|his|her) (highness|majesty)", "did I hurt your feelings", "boo hoo", "poor you", "my bad" in a serious context; elongated vowels (soooo) and "oh, I'm sorry" at start.

**21. "To be fair" / "in my defense" (S).** Examples: "To be fair, you started it." / "In my defense, I was tired." / "In fairness..." / "For what it's worth, you did too." / "I'm not the only one who..." Backfires: introduces a rebuttal in the middle of an apology, same function as "but" (support: thin; follows Lerner's "but = excuse" logic). Detect: "to be fair", "in fairness", "in my defense", "in my defence", "for what it's worth", "not the only one", "you did too", "you also", "it takes two", "both of us".

**22. Weasel hedges (W).** Examples: "I guess I'm sorry." / "Maybe I was a little out of line." / "I'm kind of sorry." / "Sort of my bad." / "I suppose I could have been nicer." / "I may have been a bit harsh." Backfires: hedges signal reluctance or partial ownership; "a little/bit" shrinks the harm (support: thin; supported by the general idea in the conditional-modifier critique of Kador via Wikipedia). Detect: hedge word (guess, suppose, maybe, perhaps, kind of, sort of, a little, a bit, somewhat, I may have, I might have, I could have, possibly, apparently) within ~4 words of a sorry/apologize or an ownership verb (was, said, did, hurt).

**23. Apologizing for the other's feelings vs. own actions (S).** Examples: "I'm sorry you're so upset." / "Sorry that you're so angry." / "I'm sorry this is hard for you." / "I'm sorry you got hurt." Backfires: same mechanism as 2, but with an adjective phrase and no verb "feel"; the subject of regret is the other's state (Deflector, ReachLink). Detect: sorry/regret + that + (you/your/he/she/they/people) + be-verb + emotion word, and "sorry (for|about) your (feelings|reaction|anger|hurt|pain)". Also "sorry for how you reacted".

**24. "I'm sorry you're hurt by my words" (S).** Examples: "I'm sorry you're hurt by my words." / "I'm sorry my comment hurt you." (second is fine) / "I'm sorry you were bothered by what I said." / "Sorry my joke upset you" (borderline, since it centers the effect with an agent). Backfires: when the sentence is built as "you were + emotion + by + my X," the other person's reaction is grammatical subject and my act is a passive-object modifier; it names the act, so it is a weaker variant of category 2. Detect: sorry + you + (are|were) + (hurt|bothered|offended|upset|affected) + "by". Keep W when it has "my [words/comment/actions]"; treat as S when "if" is also present.

**25. "Sorry not sorry" (S).** Examples: "Sorry not sorry." / "I'm sorry, not sorry." / "Not sorry." / "I'm not sorry, but..." / "I'm only sorry that I got caught." Backfires: explicitly disclaims remorse. Detect: "sorry not sorry", "not (even )?sorry", "i'm not sorry", "only sorry (that|you|because)", "sorry (that )?i got caught", "sorry i('m| am) not sorry".

**26. Corporate non-apologies (S).** Examples: "We regret any inconvenience this may have caused." / "We apologize to anyone who may have been offended." / "We are sorry if our message was misinterpreted." / "Mistakes were made, and we are looking into it." / "We take this very seriously." / "Our values are not reflected in this." / "We apologize for any confusion." Backfires: generic audience, conditional "any/may", regret instead of apology, no named failure or fix (PR sources; Wikipedia "generic audience"). Detect: "any inconvenience", "any confusion", "any (distress|trouble|misunderstanding)", "may have (caused|been|offended|upset)", "anyone who (may|might)", "to those (who|that) (may|might|were)", "we regret", "we take (this|your) .* seriously", "our (values|standards) (do not|don't)", "does not reflect (who we are|our values)", "was not our intent", "we are looking into". Mostly relevant to organizational messages; the rest of this app is personal apologies, so these could be W for a personal-apology tool.

**27. Regret instead of apology (W).** Examples: "I regret that this happened." / "It's regrettable." / "I wish things had gone differently." / "Unfortunately, this occurred." Backfires: expresses feelings about an outcome, not ownership of the action ("a regret is about how you feel, an apology is about how somebody else feels" per PR commentary). Detect: "I regret that", "regrettably", "regrettable", "unfortunately", "I wish (that )?(things|it|this)", with no "I (did|said|was)" ownership in the same sentence.

**28. Backhanded ownership / blame-by-comparison (W).** Examples: "I'm sorry I'm not as perfect as you." / "Sorry I'm not a mind reader." / "I'm sorry you expect so much of me." (support: thin; my observation). Detect: "sorry (that )?(i'm|i am) not", "sorry you (expect|think|want|need)", "sorry (that )?you (can't|couldn't|won't)". Overlaps with 20 and 2; strong beats weak on overlap.

**29. Counter-accusations in the same message (W).** Examples: "I'm sorry, and you've never apologized either." / "I apologized, now you do something." / "I'm sorry I snapped, you were rude too." Backfires: same as reciprocity plus blame. Detect: sorry sentence followed in the next sentence by "you" + (didn't|never|also|too|always).

**30. Generic-audience / third-party apology ("to anyone who...", "for the people who...") (S).** Examples: "I apologize to anyone I may have hurt." / "Sorry to everyone who was affected." / "I'm sorry for any pain I caused to those involved." Backfires: not addressed to the injured party (Wikipedia). Detect: "to anyone", "to everyone who", "to those who", "anyone who", "whoever", "for any (pain|hurt|offense|harm)".

Implementation notes for a regex-style checker (words only): normalize case and curly apostrophes; treat "sorry/apologize/apologise/apologies/regret/my bad/pardon" as an anchor term; look for windows of ~0-8 words after the anchor for categories 1-4, 23-24; allow optional "really/so/truly/very/deeply/terribly" intensifiers between "sorry" and what follows; handle contractions (I'm/I am/Im); allow "that" optional; run sentence-level so a "but" after a period still counts; strong flags suppress overlapping weak ones; show each as a question ("Does this put the reaction on the other person?").
